The shape of a story. Most satisfying stories follow an arc: a beginning that establishes a character and their ordinary world, a rising middle where a problem grows and complications mount, a climax where everything comes to a head, and a resolution that shows how the character and their world have changed. Even a short tale feels complete when it moves from a want, through obstacles, to a changed ending.

Show, don't tell. Instead of stating that a character is frightened, let the reader feel it through action and detail — a dropped cup, a held breath, a shadow at the window. Concrete, sensory description invites readers to experience a scene rather than be informed about it. Telling has its place for pace and summary, but the vivid moments that stay with a reader are almost always shown.

Character wants and needs. A compelling character is driven by a want — an outward goal they pursue — and often a deeper need they may not recognise. The tension between the two powers many stories: a character chases the thing they think they want and discovers what they truly needed. Give a character a clear desire and an obstacle, and a story begins to move on its own.

Conflict is the engine. Without conflict there is no story, only description. Conflict can be a struggle against another person, against nature or circumstance, against society, or within the character's own heart. Raising the stakes — making success matter more and failure cost more — keeps a reader turning pages. The obstacle should grow harder, not easier, as the story goes on.

Point of view. Who tells the story shapes everything. First person ("I") is intimate and immediate but limited to one mind. Third person can stay close to a single character or roam freely between many. Choosing a viewpoint means choosing what the reader can know and feel; the best choice is the one that best serves the emotional heart of the tale.

Beginnings that hook. A strong opening drops the reader into something already in motion — a moment of change, a question, a striking image or voice — rather than a slow throat-clearing of background. You can trust readers to catch up. Curiosity is the hook: give them a person to care about and a small mystery, and they will follow you down the rabbit-hole.

Dialogue. Good dialogue does more than convey information; it reveals character, hints at what is unspoken, and carries its own small conflicts. People rarely say exactly what they mean, and a pause or a change of subject can speak volumes. Read dialogue aloud to test whether it sounds like a living voice rather than a lecture.

Metaphor and simile. A simile compares two things using "like" or "as" — the mist lay like a blanket. A metaphor states the comparison outright — the mist was a blanket. Both make the abstract vivid by linking it to the familiar. The freshest images surprise a little; tired comparisons that everyone has heard before (a cliché) slide past unnoticed.

Editing and revision. First drafts are for discovering what you want to say; revision is where writing truly happens. Cut what does not earn its place, prefer strong verbs to piles of adjectives, and read your work aloud to hear its rhythm and catch clumsy lines. It often helps to set a draft aside for a day so you return to it as a reader rather than its author.

Imagination and "what if". Many wonderful stories begin with a simple question: what if a girl followed a talking rabbit, what if a house could walk, what if today repeated forever? Take something ordinary and change one rule of the world, then follow the consequences honestly. Nonsense and fantasy work best when they obey their own logic, however strange.

Pacing and tension. Vary the rhythm of a story as you would music: quick, clipped sentences quicken a scene; longer, flowing ones slow it for reflection. Withholding information creates suspense, while a well-placed revelation releases it. Give readers moments to breathe between intense scenes, and end chapters on a small pull that makes them want to read on.

Theme. Theme is what a story is really about beneath its events — an idea about courage, or change, or belonging. The best themes are shown through what happens rather than announced, and they emerge from the choices characters make under pressure. You need not decide your theme before you begin; often you discover it in the writing and sharpen it in revision.
