Deduction versus induction. Deduction reasons from general rules to a certain conclusion: if all men are mortal and Socrates is a man, then Socrates is mortal. Induction reasons from specific observations to a probable general rule, as when repeated sunrises lead us to expect another. Much practical detective reasoning is really abduction — inference to the best explanation — choosing the hypothesis that most simply accounts for all the evidence.

Observation before inference. Most people see without observing. Reasoning is only as good as the facts it rests on, so gather details first and interpret second. Note what is present and, just as importantly, what is absent — the dog that did not bark tells you the intruder was no stranger. Train yourself to record small particulars others overlook: wear on a sleeve, mud of a certain colour, the set of a person's hands.

Eliminating the impossible. When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth. Work by ruling out explanations that the evidence forbids, and do not discard a surprising answer merely because it seems unlikely. Improbable is not the same as impossible; only contradiction with the facts truly excludes a theory.

The danger of theorising too soon. It is a capital mistake to theorise before you have data, for one begins to twist facts to suit theories instead of theories to suit facts. Hold your conclusion loosely until the evidence is in. A good investigator lets the facts lead and is willing to abandon a favoured idea the moment a single stubborn fact refuses to fit it.

Occam's razor. Among competing explanations that fit the evidence equally well, prefer the simplest — the one that requires the fewest assumptions. This principle, often called Occam's razor, is a guide, not a law: reality is sometimes complicated. But a plain explanation that covers all the facts should be overturned only by evidence a simpler story cannot account for.

Breaking a problem into steps. A tangled problem yields when broken into a chain of small, clear questions, each answered from evidence before the next is asked. State what you know, what you need to find, and what would distinguish one explanation from another. Reasoning backwards — starting from the result and asking what must have produced it — is a powerful and underused skill.

Base rates and probability. To judge how likely a conclusion is, weigh not only the evidence but how common the cause is to begin with. A rare disease remains unlikely even after a positive test if false positives outnumber true cases. Ignoring these base rates is a frequent error; a careful reasoner asks "how often does this happen at all?" before trusting a striking clue.

Correlation is not causation. Two things that occur together need not be cause and effect; both may follow from a third, hidden cause, or the link may be coincidence. Before claiming that one thing produced another, ask whether the timing fits, whether a mechanism is plausible, and whether the pattern holds when other factors are accounted for.

Cognitive biases to guard against. The mind favours evidence that confirms what it already believes and overlooks what contradicts it — confirmation bias. It clings to first impressions (anchoring), overweights vivid recent events (availability), and sees patterns in randomness. Knowing these traps is the first defence: actively seek the evidence that would prove you wrong.

Testing a hypothesis. A theory earns belief by making predictions that could fail and then surviving the test. Ask of any explanation: what would I expect to observe if this were true, and what would I never observe? Then go and look. The explanation that forbids the most and is still not contradicted is the strongest.

Reasoning from small clues. Details reveal history. Calluses, stains, accents, and habits are the sediment a life leaves behind, and read together they narrow the field of possibilities. No single clue proves much, but a convergence of independent small facts, each pointing the same way, builds a conclusion far stronger than any one of them alone.

Logical fallacies. Beware arguments that attack the person rather than the claim (ad hominem), that assume what they set out to prove (circular reasoning), that offer only two options when more exist (false dilemma), or that appeal to popularity or authority in place of evidence. A conclusion can be false even when it feels persuasive; test the structure of the argument, not its confidence.
