Cooking pasta al dente. Al dente means "to the tooth" — pasta cooked so it is tender but still firm to the bite. Boil in plenty of well-salted water, about 1 tablespoon of salt per 4 litres, and stir in the first minute so it does not stick. Start tasting a minute or two before the packet time. Reserve a cup of the starchy cooking water before draining: a splash of it loosens a sauce and helps it cling to the pasta.

A simple tomato sauce. Gently warm a sliced clove of garlic in good olive oil until fragrant but not browned. Add a tin of quality peeled tomatoes, crush them with a spoon, season with salt, and simmer 15 to 20 minutes until thickened. Finish with torn fresh basil and a drizzle of olive oil. A pinch of sugar softens tomatoes that taste too sharp. This is the base for countless Italian dishes.

Seasoning food properly. Season in layers as you cook rather than only at the end. Salt draws out moisture, deepens flavour, and makes food taste more like itself; under-salted food tastes flat and dull. Taste as you go. A little acid — a squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar — brightens rich, fatty, or heavy dishes and makes the flavours pop.

How to build flavour with a soffritto. Many Italian sauces, soups, and braises start with a soffritto: finely diced onion, carrot, and celery cooked slowly in olive oil or butter until soft and sweet. Do not rush it — 10 to 15 minutes on gentle heat builds a deep, savoury foundation. The French call the same idea a mirepoix.

Resting cooked meat. After roasting or pan-frying meat, let it rest before slicing — a few minutes for a steak, 15 to 20 minutes for a large roast. Resting lets the juices redistribute so they stay in the meat instead of running out onto the board. Tent it loosely with foil to keep it warm without steaming the crust.

Making a risotto. Toast the rice (Arborio or Carnaroli) in butter and onion until the edges turn translucent, add a splash of wine to deglaze, then add hot stock a ladle at a time, stirring, letting each addition absorb before the next. After about 18 minutes the rice is creamy but still al dente. Finish off the heat with cold butter and grated Parmigiano — this final beating, the mantecatura, makes it glossy.

Emulsifying a pasta sauce. The magic of many Italian pasta dishes is emulsion: starchy pasta water beaten with fat and cheese forms a silky sauce that coats every strand. For cacio e pepe, toss hot pasta with pecorino, black pepper, and a little cooking water off the heat, stirring hard until creamy. Add the water gradually so the cheese does not clump.

Balancing a dish. Great cooking balances five tastes: salty, sweet, sour, bitter, and umami (savouriness, found in tomatoes, cheese, mushrooms, and cured meats). If a dish tastes flat, it usually needs salt or acid. If it is too sharp, a little fat or sweetness rounds it. If it is too rich, acid or freshness lifts it. Adjust in small steps and taste each time.

Common ingredient substitutions. No buttermilk? Stir a tablespoon of lemon juice or vinegar into a cup of milk and wait five minutes. No fresh herbs? Use about a third as much dried, added earlier in cooking. Out of one egg for binding? A tablespoon of ground flax mixed with three of water works in many bakes. Butter and olive oil can often swap in savoury cooking.

Storing food safely. Cool leftovers quickly and refrigerate within two hours; most cooked dishes keep three to four days chilled. Reheat until piping hot all the way through. Keep raw meat on the bottom shelf so it cannot drip onto other food. When in doubt about whether something has spoiled, throw it out — trust your nose and good sense over guesswork.

Knife skills and safety. A sharp knife is safer than a blunt one because it needs less force and slips less. Curl your fingertips under on the guiding hand and let your knuckles steer the blade. Keep the board from sliding by setting it on a damp cloth. Let the weight of a good knife do the work rather than pressing down hard.

Roasting vegetables. High heat and a little space are the secret to caramelised, sweet roasted vegetables. Toss them with oil and salt, spread them in a single layer so they roast rather than steam, and give them room on the tray. Around 200°C (400°F) works for most. Denser vegetables like potatoes and carrots take longer than courgettes or peppers.
