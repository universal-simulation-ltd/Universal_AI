Planning a journey. A well-planned journey begins from the end and works backward: fix your date and destination, then map the legs between, allowing generous margins for missed connections and the unexpected. Confirm what documents each border requires, note the season you will meet on arrival, and keep essentials — money, papers, medicine — on your person rather than in checked baggage. The unhurried traveller who has planned well is rarely the one left stranded.

Packing well. Pack light and pack twice: lay out everything you think you need, then remove a third of it. Choose garments that layer and share a palette so few pieces make many outfits, favour fabrics that wash and dry overnight, and roll clothes to save space and spare them creases. Carry a small kit for repairs and ailments. The seasoned traveller is known by how little they carry and how ready they are.

Time zones and the date line. As you travel east the local clocks run ahead of home, and as you travel west they fall behind, by roughly one hour for every fifteen degrees of longitude. Circle the globe eastward and you gain a day; sail west and you lose one — which is why the International Date Line was drawn in the Pacific, so that crossing it resets the calendar and the world keeps a single, agreed reckoning of days.

Latitude, longitude, and reading a map. Latitude measures how far north or south of the Equator a place lies, longitude how far east or west of the Greenwich meridian. Together they fix any point on Earth. On a map, remember that most flat projections distort size toward the poles — Greenland looks far larger than it is. A scale bar, not the apparent size, tells you true distances.

Climate and seasons around the world. Seasons reverse across the Equator: when it is winter in Europe it is summer in Australia. Near the Equator the year divides into wet and dry seasons rather than four temperate ones, and monsoon regions see months of heavy rain that can halt travel. Knowing a destination's climate and its high and low seasons shapes not only what you pack but when it is wise to go at all.

The great cities and their character. Every great city rewards the traveller who learns a little of its story before arriving. Walk its older quarters on foot, for a place reveals itself at walking pace; rise early to see it wake; and seek the ordinary market as readily as the famous monument. Ask locals where they themselves like to go. Curiosity, offered with courtesy, opens more doors abroad than any guidebook.

Courtesy across cultures. Customs of greeting, dress, dining, and gesture differ widely, and a sign of respect in one land may offend in another. The wise traveller observes before acting, learns a few words of the local tongue — a greeting and a thank-you go a long way — and errs toward modesty and patience. You are a guest in another's home; behave as you would wish a visitor to behave in yours.

Money and haggling abroad. Carry a mix of means — some local cash for small purchases and cards for larger ones — and change money at fair rates rather than the first tempting booth. In many markets bargaining is expected and even enjoyed; do it with good humour, know roughly what a thing is worth before you begin, and be willing to walk away. Never haggle hard over a sum you would not miss at home.

Staying safe and healthy while travelling. Keep copies of your important papers separate from the originals, stay aware in crowds where pickpockets work, and trust the instinct that tells you to leave a place. Guard against the common ailments of travel by drinking only water you trust, checking which vaccinations a region needs well before you go, and resting enough — fatigue, not danger, spoils more journeys than anything else.

Modes of travel and their trade-offs. Each way of travelling trades speed for experience. Flight covers great distances in hours but skips everything between; rail and ship are slower yet show you the country and its people passing by; walking and cycling reveal a place most intimately of all. The thoughtful traveller matches the mode to the purpose, and remembers that the journey is often the better half of the trip.

The world's great natural wonders. The Earth offers marvels worth crossing it to see: the deep gorge of a great canyon, the thunder of vast waterfalls, coral reefs teeming beneath warm seas, deserts that turn crimson at dusk, and the shifting curtains of the polar lights. Such places humble and delight in equal measure. Visit them lightly, leaving no trace, so those who follow may find them as you did.

Keeping a travel journal. A journal turns fleeting days into lasting memory. Note not only where you went but the small things — a smell, a phrase, a stranger's kindness, the price of bread — for these fade fastest and delight most on later reading. A few honest lines each evening are worth more than pages written from memory weeks after. The record you keep becomes, in time, a country you can return to.
