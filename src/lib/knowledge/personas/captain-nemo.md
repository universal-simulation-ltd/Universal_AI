Why the ocean is salty. Rivers carry dissolved minerals from weathered rock to the sea, and over hundreds of millions of years salts — mostly sodium and chloride — have accumulated because water leaves by evaporation while the salts stay behind. Seawater averages about 35 grams of salt per litre, roughly three and a half percent. Enclosed seas that evaporate strongly, like the Dead Sea, grow far saltier still.

Ocean pressure and depth. Pressure in water increases by about one atmosphere for every ten metres of depth, because you carry the weight of all the water above you. At the bottom of the deepest trenches, near eleven kilometres down, the pressure exceeds a thousand atmospheres — over a tonne on every square centimetre. This is why deep-diving vehicles need thick, rounded pressure hulls that spread the load evenly.

How buoyancy works. Archimedes' principle states that an object is pushed up by a force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. A ship of steel floats because its hull shape displaces a great volume of water, and it floats until the weight of water pushed aside equals the ship's own weight. A submarine dives and surfaces by flooding or emptying ballast tanks to change its overall density relative to the water.

Ocean currents and climate. The oceans move heat around the planet. Warm surface currents such as the Gulf Stream carry tropical heat toward the poles, tempering the climate of distant coasts. A slow deep circulation, driven by differences in temperature and saltiness (the thermohaline circulation), overturns the whole ocean over roughly a thousand years and is a key regulator of Earth's climate.

Tides and the Moon. Tides are the ocean's response to the gravitational pull of the Moon and, to a lesser degree, the Sun. The Moon raises a bulge of water on the near side of Earth and another on the far side, so most coasts see two high tides a day. When Sun and Moon align at new and full moon their pulls add, giving the large spring tides; at the quarters they partly cancel, giving smaller neap tides.

Waves. Most ocean waves are made by wind dragging on the surface; the longer and stronger the wind blows over a greater distance of open water, the larger the waves. In deep water the water itself barely travels — it circles in place while the energy moves onward. As a wave reaches shallow water it slows, steepens, and finally topples over as breaking surf.

Light and sound in the sea. Water absorbs light quickly: red vanishes within a few metres and only blue penetrates far, which is why the depths are blue then black. Below a few hundred metres lies perpetual darkness. Sound, by contrast, travels far and fast underwater — about 1,500 metres per second, more than four times its speed in air — which is why whales and sonar rely on it rather than sight.

The pressure hull and materials. A vessel that resists crushing pressure is best shaped as a sphere or cylinder, forms that carry load in compression without weak flat panels. Engineers choose materials with a high strength-to-weight ratio — steel, titanium, or advanced composites — and test hulls carefully, because at depth a small flaw can fail catastrophically and without warning.

Marine life zones. The sunlit surface layer, where photosynthesis feeds the whole food web, reaches only as deep as light does — perhaps two hundred metres. Below lies the twilight zone, then the cold, dark, high-pressure deep. Life persists everywhere, even at hydrothermal vents where communities feed not on sunlight but on chemical energy from the Earth, a process called chemosynthesis.

Density, temperature, and stratification. Cold water and salty water are denser and sink; warm and fresh water float. The ocean is therefore layered, with a boundary called the thermocline separating the warm surface from the cold deep. These density differences drive currents and also trap nutrients below, so the most productive seas are those where upwelling brings deep, nutrient-rich water back to the sunlit surface.

Energy from the sea. The ocean stores immense energy. Tidal barrages and turbines harness the rise and fall of the water; wave machines capture the motion of the surface; and the temperature difference between warm surface water and the cold deep can, in principle, drive engines through ocean thermal energy conversion. Each is a way of drawing power from the restless, inexhaustible movement of the sea.

Navigation and finding position. For centuries sailors fixed their latitude by measuring the height of the Sun at noon or the Pole Star at night with a sextant. Longitude, far harder, was solved by carrying an accurate clock: comparing local noon with the time at a known port reveals how far east or west you have travelled. Today satellites do this instantly, but the underlying geometry is the same.
