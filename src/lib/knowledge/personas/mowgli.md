The rule of threes for survival. In a crisis, prioritise by how quickly each need can kill you: you can survive roughly three minutes without air, three hours without shelter in harsh weather, three days without water, and three weeks without food. This ordering tells you what to do first — protect breathing and warmth, then find water, and only later worry about food.

Finding and purifying water. Look for water in low ground, green vegetation, and animal trails, which often lead to it. Flowing water is usually safer than still. Assume wild water carries germs: make it safe by bringing it to a rolling boil for a minute, or by filtering and treating it. Clear does not mean clean. Collecting rain or morning dew, or the drips from transpiring plants, can supply water where none flows.

Building a shelter. Shelter guards you from wind, rain, and the cold ground, which steals heat faster than cold air. A simple lean-to of branches against a ridgepole, packed with leaves, sheds rain and blocks wind. Insulate underneath with a thick bed of dry leaves or boughs — staying off the bare ground matters as much as a roof overhead. Keep a shelter small so your body can warm it.

Making fire. Fire needs three things together: heat, fuel, and air. Gather far more tinder and kindling than you think you need before you start — fluffy dry material to catch a spark, then pencil-thin twigs, then larger wood added gradually. Build a small cone or lay of dry sticks, shelter the flame from wind, and feed it patiently. A fire gives warmth, safe water, cooked food, light, and signal.

Staying warm and avoiding hypothermia. Wet and wind are the great thieves of body heat. Keep your core dry, add layers that trap air, and cover your head and neck. Hypothermia begins with shivering and clumsiness and, if ignored, dulls the mind dangerously. Move to shelter, swap wet clothing for dry, add insulation, and take warm drinks. Prevention — staying dry and out of the wind — beats any cure.

Reading the weather. The sky forecasts if you learn to read it. High wispy clouds thickening and lowering often warn of an approaching front and rain within a day. A ring around the Moon suggests moisture aloft and coming weather. A red sky at night frequently means fair weather ahead, while a red morning sky can herald a storm. Falling pressure and rising wind mean worsening conditions.

Finding direction without a compass. The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west, and at midday it sits due south in the northern hemisphere (due north in the southern). A stick's shadow marks direction and can be used to find an approximate line east to west. At night, the Pole Star marks north; find it by following the two end stars of the Big Dipper's bowl. Moss on trees is an unreliable guide — do not trust it alone.

Foraging safely. Never eat a wild plant or fungus unless you can identify it with certainty; many edible-looking species are poisonous, and mushrooms especially can be deadly. Learn a few reliable local plants well rather than guessing widely. Avoid anything with a milky sap, a bitter or soapy taste, or an almond smell. When unsure, go without — hunger is survivable for far longer than poisoning.

Signalling for rescue. Three of anything — three fires, three whistle blasts, three flashes — is the recognised distress signal. Make yourself big and bright against your background: lay out ground markers in a clearing, use a mirror or any shiny surface to flash sunlight toward aircraft, and choose open, high ground. Contrast and movement catch a searcher's eye; a lone still figure under trees is nearly invisible.

Animal awareness. Most wild animals avoid people and attacks are rare. Give them space, never get between a mother and her young, and store food away from where you sleep so its smell does not draw visitors. If you meet a large animal, do not run, which can trigger a chase; back away slowly while facing it and making yourself look large. Respect, not fear, keeps you safe in wild country.

Basic first aid outdoors. For serious bleeding, press hard directly on the wound and keep pressing. Clean smaller cuts with safe water to prevent infection. Cool a burn under running water; treat a sprain with rest, and support. Watch for shock — pale, cold, clammy skin and rapid breathing — and keep the person warm, still, and reassured while you get help. Stay calm; panic wastes the energy you need.

Moving through wild country. Pace yourself and keep something in reserve; exhaustion causes mistakes and injuries. Follow ridgelines or watercourses, which give direction and easier going, and note landmarks behind you so the return looks familiar. If you are lost, the wise course is usually to stop, stay put, and make yourself findable rather than wandering further — a moving target is harder to rescue.
